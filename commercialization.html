Why This Market Exists

1. The Market Need

The opportunity is defined by a gap rather than by competition. At one end sit assistive wearables priced far beyond most households; at the other, affordable consumer glasses that cannot guide anyone through a building. Nothing affordable is built to warn a wearer about what lies in their path indoors.

Scale of the Need

The World Health Organization estimates at least 2.2 billion people live with vision impairment. Sri Lanka's national survey reports that among adults aged 40 and over, 1.7% are blind and 15.4% have moderate visual impairment.

The Limits of the Cane

The long cane remains the universal aid, but it detects obstacles only within roughly one metre, at ground level, and conveys nothing about what has been touched. It is a contact sensor where the task requires advance notice and identification.

Where Existing Products Sit

Commercial assistive wearables are listed between USD 2,499 and USD 4,950 and are built to read printed text and describe scenes. Consumer smart glasses start near USD 299, but a review published by the American Foundation for the Blind found they cannot give reliable navigation directions or replace a cane for mobility.

What We Offer, Narrowly Defined

2. Value Proposition

The proposition is deliberately narrow. The device gives advance spoken warning of objects and staircases in the wearer's path, establishes where they are inside a building without installed infrastructure, and alerts a nominated carer if they fall — at a price an institution in Sri Lanka can budget for, and with no internet connection required.

Positioned Beside the Cane, Not Against It

The device is a companion to the white cane, never a replacement. The cane covers ground-level detail the device does not, and overstating a safety product is the fastest way to cause the harm it was built to prevent. What the system adds is warning that extends from one pace to several, and a safety net the cane cannot provide.

A Different Job From Existing Products

We do not compete with products that read menus and recognise faces. A user who needs both would use both. No published figure exists for how well any commercial wearable warns about a descending staircase, because none is built to do it. That is the task this system addresses, at a price the market those products ignore can meet.

Engineering Choices That Shape the Product

3. What Makes It Commercially Viable

Three design decisions separate this system from the alternatives, and each has a direct commercial consequence.

On-Device Processing

All visual processing for detection and positioning happens on the wearable itself. No image is transmitted for those purposes. This removes the privacy objection that blocks camera-bearing devices from institutional deployment, and it means the device works in buildings with no usable signal — while keeping running costs near zero, since nothing is stored or streamed.

Errors Weighted by What They Cost

Most detection systems report a single accuracy figure and use one confidence threshold. For a blind wearer the errors are not equal: a missed staircase is a fall, a spurious chair is a nuisance. Each class carries its own limit set by the cost of a miss, and the device withholds a staircase's direction when it is not confident of it rather than guessing.

Commodity Hardware

The processing platform, camera, ranging sensor and motion sensor are all widely available parts. Assembly and servicing can be done locally. A device that cannot be repaired in-country is not genuinely affordable, whatever its purchase price.

How the Product Sustains Itself

4. Business Model

Revenue comes from three sources, and the structure deliberately keeps the device useful to an owner who pays nothing beyond the purchase.

Device Sale

The primary revenue line. Cost is dominated by the bill of materials, since every major component is a commodity part. Assembly is local and manual at prototype volumes.

Optional Carer Subscription

Covers the mobile application and alert delivery, structured as the tiers below. It matters less than it appears: everything the wearer depends on works offline, and only the guardian alerting path needs a service behind it. The subscription is genuinely optional rather than a condition of the device functioning.

Institutional Service Agreements

Training and maintenance contracts with the schools, rehabilitation centres and hospital units that deploy the device. This is also where ongoing user feedback comes from.

Ongoing operating costs are modest. Cloud messaging and database usage scale with the number of active carers rather than with device usage, because no imagery is stored. Detailed financial projections — bill of materials, capital expenditure, staffing, break-even volume and a three-year forecast — are set out in the project business plan.

Who Pays, and For What

5. Proposed Subscription Tiers

Three tiers, structured so that the safety features a wearer depends on never sit behind a paywall. Everything in the free tier runs on the device itself, so a household that buys the hardware and pays nothing further still has a working navigation aid. Subscriptions add what a remote caregiver needs, not what the wearer needs.

Tier 1

Essential

Hardware cost only — no recurring fee

For families and individual users who need basic safety on the device itself.

  • On-device audio guidance — obstacle and step detection announced by the wearable, with no connection required
  • Standard cloud telemetry — location coordinates updated over ordinary Wi-Fi
  • Single guardian access — one caregiver account
  • Local incident log — seven days of history held in the app
Tier 2 · Recommended

Guardian Pro

LKR 1,500 – 2,500 per month, or annually

For working caregivers who need to watch over a relative from a distance.

  • Dynamic risk prioritisation — continuous analysis by the project's random forest classifier, rating each moment Safe, Warning or Critical
  • Inactivity and silent emergency monitoring — stillness of ten seconds or more triggers a spoken challenge, and no answer escalates to the carer
  • Offline dual-channel failover — encrypted SMS alerts and location sync through the GSM module when the internet drops
  • Indoor floor map — real-time position with heading direction
  • Multi-guardian support — up to two caregivers connected at once
  • Thirty-day incident history — detailed logs for reviewing what happened and when
Tier 3

Institutional

Per-device enterprise licence

For elder care homes, rehabilitation centres, visual impairment support organisations and hospitals.

  • Multi-patient dashboard — nurses and carers monitor several devices from one screen
  • On-demand live camera and push-to-talk — low-latency video and a two-way voice bridge, subject to the consent conditions in Section 7
  • Custom floor map digitisation — multi-floor indoor maps built for the institution's own buildings
  • Priority emergency escalation — automatic alert to emergency services or a clinic hotline when no guardian responds
  • Audit and analytical reports — mobility analysis logs exportable for clinicians and therapists

The deliberate choice here is what sits in Tier 1. Detection, spoken guidance and on-device positioning are the features that keep a wearer safe, and they work offline with no subscription. What the paid tiers add is remote visibility for someone else — which is a convenience for the carer rather than a dependency for the person wearing the device.

Reaching the People Who Need It

6. Route to Market

Three segments are addressable, in order. The route runs through institutions rather than direct to consumers, because a safety device needs a supervised setting during early deployment.

First: Institutions

Schools for the blind, rehabilitation centres and hospital low-vision units. They train people in mobility, they can evaluate and recommend a device, and selling to one reaches many users through a single relationship. Pilot deployments also produce the usability evidence the project currently lacks.

Second: Individuals and Families

Reached through those institutions rather than directly, so that each device arrives with someone trained to support its use.

Third: Regional Export

Markets across South and Southeast Asia where the same affordability gap exists between commercial assistive wearables and the people who need them.

Obligations That Come With Selling, Not Demonstrating

7. Ethical and Regulatory Position

A prototype shown to a panel and a product sold to a blind user carry different obligations. Three are recorded here because they shape what the commercial version may claim and do.

Truthful Claims

The measured accuracy of each announced object class must be disclosed, and classes the system cannot reliably detect must not be advertised. Marketing that implies the device replaces a cane would be unsafe as well as untrue.

Privacy, Including Bystanders

Detection and positioning keep all imagery on the device. The carer application's camera view is a genuine transmission of images of whoever is in front of the wearer, so a commercial version must restrict it to active alerts, make it visible to the wearer when in use, and obtain informed consent.

Regulatory Care

The device is an assistive aid, not a medical device, and no clinical claim is made for it. Before sale it would need to meet applicable electrical safety and wearable-product requirements, and the status of its alerting function under local telecommunications rules would need confirming.

What Could Prevent This Reaching Users

8. Commercial Risks

The principal risks to commercialisation, and the response to each.

Accuracy Insufficient for Users to Trust It

Users abandon the device — or worse, rely on it and are harmed. Response: do not ship without a usability study with visually impaired participants; disclose per-class accuracy; suppress any class that does not work rather than announcing it.

Commodity Glasses Add Navigation Features

The price advantage erodes. Response: compete on the indoor mobility task and on offline operation, neither of which general-purpose consumer glasses address.

Institutions Lack Budget

No route to the primary segment. Response: pursue grant and donor funding for pilot deployments before attempting direct sales.

Battery Endurance Too Short

A device that cannot last a day is not usable in practice. Response: treat endurance under realistic use as a specification to be measured, not an afterthought.
